The AI Subscription Dilemma: Are Premium Models Worth Your Money?

My personal journey through the evolving AI landscape and why these tools are headed toward utility status PART 2:

As someone deeply immersed in both research and development work, I've had a front-row seat to the rapidly evolving AI assistant market. I want to share my experiences with you and explain why I believe we're witnessing the early stages of AI commoditization.

My $400 Investment in ChatGPT - Was It Worth It?

I paid ChatGPT $200 dollars a month for two months and I made good use of it. During that time I used it to help me research and develop the math for three scientific papers that I published. Was it worth it? Absolutely.

The premium subscription gave me access to more advanced reasoning capabilities that proved invaluable while working through complex mathematical concepts. The model was able to help me refine my approach, catch errors in my formulations, and suggest alternative mathematical frameworks when I hit roadblocks.

For serious academic work, having an AI assistant that could keep up with specialized terminology and complex reasoning made a substantial difference in my productivity. Those three papers might have taken twice as long without this tool at my disposal, making the $400 investment seem quite reasonable in retrospect.

However, as with any technology investment, needs change, and what seems essential today may not remain so tomorrow.

Pivoting from Research to Development

This month I switched from the ChatGPT Pro plan to the ChatGPT Plus plan. While it felt essential at the time, given my needs then, I've shifted from writing papers to writing code. My needs have changed, and the best AI coding model from OpenAI is GPT 4.1 & it's API only.

This transition highlighted something important: as powerful as these models are, they each have distinct strengths and weaknesses. While ChatGPT excelled at helping me work through mathematical concepts and draft academic content, I found myself wanting to explore alternatives for coding assistance.

I've been hearing a ton of good things about Google's new Gemini 2.5 Pro & Flash for coding, and after just a few days of coding I made significant progress on my long-term A-Frame WebXR project, but I'm now painfully aware of its limitations and faults.

Despite the painful limitations of Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro, I decided that it was worth paying the monthly service plan of about $20 dollars in addition to paying for ChatGPT at $20 dollars, and I don't think I will exhaust my daily allotment for either model.

This dual subscription approach might seem excessive to some, but having access to multiple AI perspectives has proven invaluable for my development work. Where one model fails to understand a particular framework or approach, the other often succeeds. The combined power of these tools has accelerated my WebXR project in ways that would have been difficult to achieve with human assistance alone at a comparable price point.

The Coming Commoditization of Advanced AI

From what people are saying plus what I have tried it seems that Google's Gemini, Anthropic's Claude, OpenAI's ChatGPT, xAI Grok, and DeepSeek are all similar in their capabilities, and similarly flawed in their limitations. AI will become like a commodities business.

Within a few years humanity will think of these names "Google's Gemini, Anthropic's Claude, OpenAI's ChatGPT, xAI Grok, and DeepSeek" like they think of the names of cable companies, tv channels, or electricity companies, Comcast, PG&E, Edison, HBO, etc... A basic utility.

What we're witnessing is the early stage of what will eventually become a utilities market. The core capabilities of these models will continue to converge, with each perhaps maintaining some specialty areas but largely offering similar fundamental services. The differentiators will become less about raw capability and more about integration, pricing, reliability, and specialized features.

Trump's administration is causing small companies to be eaten up by larger companies, and that's one trend, but AI will provide smaller companies with new ways to compete with larger companies, and that's the larger reversing trend.

The commoditization of AI services will democratize access to these powerful tools, potentially leveling the playing field between major corporations and nimble startups. While the current administration's policies may favor consolidation in many sectors, the widespread availability of powerful AI tools creates new opportunities for disruption and innovation from unexpected sources.

PART 3: The Struggle Against Commodification

Despite the seeming inevitability of AI commoditization, the major players are fighting hard against this future. Each platform is developing strategies to make their AI assistants irreplaceable in your workflow and create psychological barriers to switching services.

OpenAI has been particularly aggressive with this approach. Their "custom GPTs" feature allows users to create specialized versions of ChatGPT tailored to specific tasks, complete with knowledge bases and custom instructions. The more time you invest in crafting these personalized assistants, the less likely you are to abandon ship for a competitor.

Similarly, Claude's memory features allow it to recall details from past conversations, creating an AI that seemingly "knows you" over time. Google's Gemini is deeply integrated with your Google Workspace, leveraging your emails, documents, and calendar to provide contextually relevant assistance that would be difficult for competitors to replicate without access to that data.

I've experienced this lock-in effect firsthand. After spending weeks fine-tuning how I interact with ChatGPT for coding assistance, I found myself hesitant to fully commit to Gemini despite its superior performance in certain areas. The familiarity and accumulated knowledge ChatGPT had about my coding style and project requirements created an invisible barrier to switching.

This is precisely what these companies want. They understand that raw AI capabilities are rapidly approaching parity. The race is now about who can make their AI feel most like "your AI" – a digital extension of yourself that becomes more valuable the longer you use it.

The Multi-AI Approach

Despite these lock-in attempts, I've found that maintaining subscriptions to multiple AI services is currently the optimal approach for serious work. Different models still have distinct strengths and weaknesses that make them complementary rather than redundant.

I recently encountered a particularly challenging WebXR development issue involving complex 3D transformations. After hitting a wall with ChatGPT's suggestions, I presented the identical problem to Gemini and received a completely different approach that ultimately solved my issue. This experience convinced me that having access to multiple AI perspectives is worth the combined subscription cost.

This multi-AI strategy is likely a temporary phase in the market's evolution. As capabilities continue to converge, maintaining multiple subscriptions will become increasingly difficult to justify – especially as these services inevitably increase their prices to compensate for the massive computational resources they require.

For now, however, I'm finding tremendous value in leveraging the unique strengths of different models. ChatGPT excels at understanding complex code architecture and providing clean, well-commented solutions. Gemini seems to have a deeper understanding of newer web technologies and frameworks. Claude offers superior reasoning for debugging complex logical problems.

Together, these tools form a powerful development ecosystem that has dramatically accelerated my productivity. The combined monthly cost of around $40 represents a fraction of what hiring human assistance would cost for similar capabilities.

The Coming Price War

As technical differentiation becomes increasingly difficult, we're likely to see competition shift toward pricing models. We're already witnessing this with Claude offering more generous token limits than ChatGPT, and Gemini providing substantial free usage before requiring payment.

This competition will intensify as more players enter the market. DeepSeek and smaller AI providers are already positioning themselves as more cost-effective alternatives to the established leaders. As training and operating costs continue to decrease through algorithmic improvements and specialized hardware, there will be increasing pressure to pass these savings on to consumers.

The current subscription model may eventually give way to usage-based pricing similar to cloud computing resources, where you pay only for what you use rather than a flat monthly fee. This would further cement the utility-like nature of these services and likely benefit power users who need intense but intermittent AI assistance.

For now, I'm continuing my dual subscription approach, extracting maximum value from both ChatGPT and Gemini. The productivity gains far outweigh the costs, and I've found that having access to multiple AI perspectives has improved not just my output but also my understanding of the problems I'm trying to solve.

Part 4: The Reality of AI Coding Limitations

Despite the impressive capabilities that initially drew me to Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash for my A-Frame WebXR project, I've encountered frustrating and persistent limitations that highlight just how far these models still need to evolve.

The problems are surprisingly basic and repetitive. Gemini consistently makes the same syntactical errors with A-Frame code, including illegal HTML comments inside A-Frame entities. I'll correct these errors, only to watch the AI make the identical mistake in its next suggestion. It's like teaching someone who refuses to learn.

Here's a particularly maddening pattern: I'll provide working code samples and explicitly instruct the AI to reference them rather than continuing to iterate on non-working approaches. Yet invariably, it reverts to its problematic patterns. Even worse, it has a habit of replacing functional code with placeholder comments like "/* keep existing code here */" that actually delete the working code I needed preserved.

These aren't isolated incidents or edge cases—they're fundamental limitations in how these models interact with specialized coding frameworks. After weeks of frustration, I tried an unusual approach: instructing the AI to leave explicit reminders to itself at the end of its messages, like a developer writing notes for their future self.

For example: "AI Self-Correction Reminder: *Ensure ALL JavaScript component definitions are fully included in the <script> tag. Do NOT use placeholder comments like /* ... */. Do NOT place HTML comments `` inside A-Frame entity tags (e.g., within <a-scene> or <a-box>). Double-check component attribute syntax. Base logic on confirmed working elements from toy models."

While this approach showed some improvement, it highlighted a fundamental truth: today's general-purpose AI models lack the specialized knowledge and consistency required for domain-specific development work like WebXR. They're missing critical context about framework conventions, best practices, and limitations.

The Future: Domain-Specific AI Models

What developers like me truly need are domain-specialized versions of these AI assistants. Imagine a Gemini or Claude variant specifically optimized for A-Frame WebXR development—one that has studied every piece of A-Frame documentation, analyzed thousands of working WebXR applications, and never makes elementary syntax errors with the framework.

These specialized models would understand the latest versions of all relevant libraries, have developed numerous working toy models demonstrating different implementation patterns, and thoroughly grasp the interactions between components. They would recognize common pitfalls and guide developers away from them rather than repeatedly suggesting broken approaches.

The current general-purpose approach is akin to having a general practitioner perform specialized surgery—they understand the basic principles but lack the specific expertise required for optimal outcomes. As AI development matures, I believe we'll see these models branch into specialized variants tailored to specific development domains.

Automating Open Source Development

Beyond mere coding assistance, I envision AI becoming a catalyst for open source development. My recent experience trying to replace the Cannon physics engine with PhysX for implementing advanced gripping functions in A-Frame illustrates this potential.

While PhysX offered the functionality I needed, its WebAssembly implementation loads painfully slowly and has proven difficult to work with. What the WebXR community really needs is a tree-shakeable JavaScript native implementation of PhysX that would allow developers to cherry-pick only the specific functions needed for their application, dramatically improving load times and performance.

Creating such an implementation manually would require hundreds of developer hours, but it's exactly the kind of task where AI could shine—systematically converting and optimizing code while maintaining functionality. AI could potentially automate the creation of optimized versions of critical open source libraries, addressing pain points that individual developers struggle to solve on their own.

This represents a profound shift in how we think about open source development. Rather than communities of human developers building libraries function by function, we might see AI systems rapidly generating optimized implementations based on functional specifications, with humans focusing on high-level architectural decisions and quality control.

For now, though, I'm still stuck copying and pasting working code samples, manually testing code, and trying to prevent my AI assistants from introducing syntax errors and omitting important code. The future is promising, especially when we solve the current problems with AI systems that will lead to closed loop software development, but the present remains annoyingly limited.

Part 5: Conclusion: Navigating the AI Transition Period

We're currently in what I consider a fascinating transitional period in AI development—one that combines both immense promise and significant frustration. The tools available today represent a quantum leap beyond what existed just a few years ago, yet they still fall short of what we know is theoretically possible.

For professionals like me who are pushing the boundaries in specialized domains like WebXR development, this reality creates a complex value proposition. I'm simultaneously amazed by what these AI assistants can help me accomplish and frustrated by their recurring limitations and blind spots.

My dual subscription approach—maintaining access to both ChatGPT and Gemini—reflects this transitional reality. No single AI solution currently meets all my needs, so I'm willing to pay roughly $40 monthly to access complementary capabilities. This represents excellent value compared to traditional development resources, even with the occasional need to work around AI limitations.

However, this doesn't mean everyone should follow the same approach. If you're working in well-established domains like web development or data analysis, the basic tiers of these services likely provide sufficient value without the premium price tags. The $200/month services are currently difficult to justify unless you're working on cutting-edge research or development where marginal improvements translate to significant time savings.

What fascinates me most is how quickly this landscape is changing. The limitations I struggle with today—like getting AI to properly handle A-Frame WebXR syntax—will likely be solved in the next generation of models. The pace of improvement is astounding, with significant advances arriving every few months rather than years.

This rapid evolution points toward the utility future I've been describing. As capabilities standardize across providers and basic competencies become table stakes rather than differentiators, we'll see increasing emphasis on pricing, reliability, and integration rather than raw capabilities.

For developers and businesses planning for this AI future, I recommend a few practical strategies:

  1. Be subscription-fluid: Don't lock yourself into long-term commitments with any single AI provider. The landscape is changing too rapidly to commit to one ecosystem.

  2. Build with migration in mind: If you're integrating AI into your workflows or products, design with the expectation that you might switch providers as the market evolves.

  3. Focus on prompt engineering skills: The ability to effectively communicate with and direct AI assistants is becoming a valuable meta-skill that transfers across platforms.

  4. Contribute to open models: Support open-source AI initiatives that are pushing toward the utility future and providing competitive alternatives to the major commercial players.

As we move further into this AI-accelerated world, I believe we'll see the emergence of a more diverse software ecosystem—one where individuals and small teams can create sophisticated, personalized solutions that previously required massive development resources. This democratization will fundamentally reshape not just how software is created, but who creates it and for whom.

The transition from today's premium AI assistants to tomorrow's commodity AI utilities won't happen overnight, but the direction is clear. What remains to be seen is how the incumbent players will adapt to this inevitable future—whether they'll embrace the utility model or fight against it with increasingly sophisticated lock-in strategies.

For now, I'll continue leveraging these tools to push forward my WebXR projects, working around their limitations while appreciating their growing capabilities. The $40 monthly investment in dual subscriptions feels reasonable during this transition period, but I don't expect this approach to remain necessary for long. As capabilities converge and specialized models emerge, the value proposition will continue to evolve.

What's certain is that we're witnessing the early days of a profound transformation in how software is created and who can create it. Despite the current limitations, the trajectory points toward a future where AI-assisted development becomes so accessible and powerful that it fundamentally reshapes the software industry—flattening hierarchies, lowering barriers to entry, and enabling a new wave of innovation from unexpected sources.

In that sense, my journey with these AI tools reflects a much larger story—one about how technology democratization eventually turns today's premium services into tomorrow's basic utilities, bringing previously elite capabilities to everyone. It's a story that's played out repeatedly throughout technological history, from electricity to computing to internet access, and now we're seeing it begin with artificial intelligence.

The only real question is how quickly we'll get there, and what amazing things we'll build along the way.
