Canva’s AI Code Generator vs. v0, Lovable, and Bolt.

Dawn of a DIY Software Revolution

aisitscience-tech

Canva has entered the AI app-building arena with a new AI code generator, aiming to make app creation as simple as designing a slide. This move positions Canva squarely against emerging tools like v0, Lovable, and Bolt, which have been gaining traction by letting users create software through natural-language prompts. In this article, we’ll detail what Canva’s tool does, how it compares to v0, Lovable, and Bolt, and who each tool is designed for. We’ll then step back and explore why so many companies are racing to build similar AI development tools – and what this trend suggests about the future trajectory of software development. Is it possible we’re headed toward a world where traditional software companies fade away, as individuals generate their own apps, websites, games, and tools using AI? We investigate this provocative hypothesis from technological, economic, and social angles, drawing on expert insights about AI-assisted software creation. Finally, we’ll venture into the realm of education and intelligence: envisioning a three-generation timeline in which AI tutors elevate human learning so dramatically that average people one day attain PhD-level knowledge. Buckle up – the implications are as bold as they are fascinating.

Canva Enters the AI App Generation Race

Canva’s announcement of its new AI-powered code generator signals that the democratization of software development is truly going mainstream. The feature, simply called Canva Code, allows users to “prompt” Canva’s AI assistant to create mini-apps – like interactive maps or custom calculators – that can be embedded into designs​. In essence, Canva users can now add functional web widgets to their presentations, websites, or marketing materials without writing a single line of code. Canva partnered with Anthropic to power this tool, leveraging the Claude large language model under the hood​. As Canva’s co-founder and CPO Cameron Adams explained, the company saw employees internally using AI to prototype interactive ideas, and decided to “externalize it and give everyone the ability to code easily and create interactive experiences”​. The goal is to let non-programmers augment their designs with real functionality, consistent with Canva’s broader mission of empowering everyone to design everything.

Importantly, Canva is not the first to roll out this kind of AI-driven app builder – far from it. Startups like Cursor, Bolt.new, Lovable, and Replit have already attracted attention by enabling users to prompt their way to creating applications​. What sets Canva’s entry apart is that it’s baked directly into a massively popular design platform. Rather than targeting professional developers, Canva Code lives alongside tools for graphics and layouts, aiming to give designers, marketers, educators, and other everyday creators a taste of “magic” code generation. It complements Canva’s evolution from a graphic design tool into an all-in-one platform for marketing content, websites, documents, and now basic apps​. As TechCrunch noted, graphic design software makers feel they must integrate AI to stay competitive​ – and Canva’s latest move underscores how quickly AI features are becoming standard, even in creative software. Canva’s bet is that by lowering the barrier to adding interactivity, it can keep users on its platform instead of losing them to more code-oriented tools. In the words of one tech blogger, “wow, Canva just launched its own AI code generator (competitor to v0, Lovable, Bolt, etc.) – things just got interesting.” The battle for AI-assisted app creation is on.

What Canva’s AI Code Generator Does (and Who It’s For)

Canva’s AI code generator is focused on simplicity and integration rather than building complex stand-alone software from scratch. Within Canva, you can now type a request in plain language – for example, “create an interactive US map that highlights each state when clicked” – and the AI will generate a working mini application fulfilling that description. The resulting widget can be placed onto a Canva design like any other element, resizing and styling it as needed​. Early demos showed things like calculators and simple games being created via prompt and dropped into Canva presentations​. The key point: no coding required from the user. Canva’s assistant writes the code (likely HTML/CSS/JS or React under the hood), and the user just interacts with the end result visually.

Because it’s integrated into Canva’s familiar drag-and-drop interface, the intended audience is non-programmers – think content creators, small business owners, teachers, and students already using Canva for graphics or docs. For these users, Canva Code adds a layer of interactivity that previously would have required hiring a developer or using separate tools. Want a custom form on your Canva-made website? Or an embedded quiz in your digital flyer? Canva is betting its users would prefer to ask the AI for it rather than seek out a standalone app builder. In this way, Canva’s AI code generator is as much a feature extension for a design platform as it is a competitor in the wider AI dev tool space. Its capabilities are likely constrained to relatively bite-sized apps and widgets that “can then be integrated in designs”​ – in other words, add-ons to Canva projects. You won’t be building the next Facebook purely inside Canva. But you could quickly whip up a tailored interactive element (map, chart, calculator, chatbot, etc.) to enhance a Canva website or presentation, which is a compelling value-add for Canva’s millions of users.

This tightly focused use case differentiates Canva’s tool from some of the more developer-oriented AI coders emerging elsewhere. Canva Code is about empowering everyone to add a pinch of software to their content, not about generating full-fledged, standalone software products. As such, it’s easy to see it as the Canva approach to AI development: make it ridiculously easy and integrated, even if that means it’s not as flexible or powerful as coding from scratch. In fact, one online discussion likened earlier AI app builders to Canva itself: “Bolt and v0 are like Canva. It’s great for everyone and gets fast results, but it’s not super customizable”​. Now Canva has quite literally become like Canva for app generation – a quick, accessible solution, intentionally streamlined for beginners and rapid results.

As the table suggests, all these tools lower the barrier to creating software, but they play to different strengths:

  • Canva Code stays true to Canva’s ethos of extreme simplicity, focusing on micro-apps that enhance creative projects. It sacrifices flexibility for ease-of-use and targets a broad, non-technical user base.

  • v0 (by Vercel) is more of a developer’s assistant: it accelerates front-end coding by producing high-quality React components from descriptions. It’s less about deploying a finished app and more about giving developers a head start that they can then integrate into a larger codebase​. In fact, v0 explicitly encourages users to “copy and paste that code into your app and develop from there” once you’re ready​. It’s a catalyst for professional coders.

  • Lovable goes broader, attempting to be a one-stop-shop for building an entire app through conversation. It handles more of the stack (even hooking up databases or external APIs on request​), and aims to deliver a deployable application that you can continuously refine. It blurs the line between no-code and traditional development – you can use it with zero coding, but if you can code, it will sync with GitHub and let you fine-tune the project in your own IDE​. This makes Lovable attractive to both non-coders aiming to launch a project and coders looking to automate the boring parts of setup.

  • Bolt.new has a similar full-app capability to Lovable, but with an emphasis on an integrated developer environment in the browser. It’s like Google Docs for app development – everything happens in-browser, collaboratively if needed, with instant execution. This lowers the friction for trying out ideas. Bolt might generate an app for you, but it expects that you’ll poke around in the code afterward (and it provides the tools to do so). In short, Bolt is geared toward developers who want speed, not escaping code entirely. It’s telling that Bolt and v0 currently favor React/JS frameworks (and have only nascent support for others)​ – they’re built with the modern web developer in mind.

Where does Canva’s AI tool fit into this competitive landscape? Canva is arguably playing in a more casual corner of the market – people whose primary work is not software development at all. The trade-off is that Canva’s generated apps will be relatively limited in scope. In contrast, v0, Lovable, and Bolt are vying to prove that serious applications can be built through AI assistance, each offering a different mix of ease vs. control. It’s worth noting that all these tools leverage powerful large language models behind the scenes (OpenAI GPT-4, Anthropic Claude, etc.), and often even share similar pricing models (freemium with token-based usage for generations​​). The rapid improvement of these underlying AI models is a rising tide lifting all boats – enabling even small startups (or a design tool company like Canva) to bolt on generative coding capabilities.

In summary, Canva’s AI code generator enters a bustling field with its own spin: integrating code generation into content creation for the masses. It won’t replace a software engineer’s IDE, but that’s exactly the point – it might replace the need for one in many small-scale scenarios. Next, let’s explore why everyone and their cousin (from big tech to startups to design platforms) is suddenly launching AI coding assistants. What’s driving this explosion of AI code generators, and what does it portend for the future of making software?

The Gold Rush for AI Code Generators: Why So Many, So Fast?

In the past two years, generative AI has gone from a novelty to an arms race in tech. The surge of AI coding tools – from giants like Microsoft’s GitHub Copilot to niche startups – is fueled by a perfect storm of technological and market forces. Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 proved astonishingly good at producing code from natural language, kicking off a wave of innovation in developer tools. Once it became clear around 2022–2023 that AI could reliably write chunks of workable code, everyone rushed to build on that capability. As Canva’s adoption shows, no company wants to be left behind. “Regardless of what their customers say, [software makers] clearly seem to think they cannot survive without implementing some form of AI,” TechCrunch observed in April 2025​. It’s a sentiment echoing across industries: integrate AI or become irrelevant.

From a business perspective, there’s immense appeal in AI-assisted development:

  • Productivity gains: Early evidence shows even basic code assist tools can boost developer productivity significantly. A Bain & Company survey found companies using generative AI for coding saw 10–15% efficiency improvements on average, and over 30% in best cases​. That’s a seismic shift in an industry always seeking faster release cycles. If a tool can cut development time by even 20%, it’s worth its weight in gold. Startups like v0, Bolt, and Lovable sense a huge market in helping startups and teams ship faster. Even more modest no-code AI tools (like Canva’s) promise productivity boosts for non-dev workflows – e.g. a marketer can create a web form without waiting on IT. Everywhere you look, AI is greasing the wheels of software creation.

  • Democratization = new markets: The no-code/low-code movement predates this AI wave, aiming to empower people to build apps without programming. Generative AI supercharges this idea by removing even the drag-and-drop learning curve – you can just explain what you want. This potentially opens up millions of new “developers” (in quotes, because they might not think of themselves as such) who can create software solutions for their own needs. Companies are racing to capture these new users. Canva, for instance, already has over 100 million users creating designs; giving them coding superpowers could unlock countless new use cases on the platform, from interactive lessons by teachers to custom calculators made by entrepreneurs. Likewise, AWS sees opportunity in enterprise folks who aren’t professional coders – hence the launch of AWS App Studio, a generative AI tool that builds entire applications from simple prompts​. Amazon’s pitch is that a business analyst or domain expert can spin up apps on AWS by describing what they need, and the AI will generate the UI, data models, and business logic​. In short, there’s a race to capture the “citizen developer” wave that generative AI is enabling.

  • Competitive pressure and FOMO: Once a few players showed what was possible – e.g. Replit demonstrated an “AI programming assistant” in early 2023, and Microsoft baked GPT-4 into Copilot – a competitive chain reaction began. Developers started expecting AI assistance in their IDEs. Entrepreneurs saw the traction of tools like v0 (which had a 100k waitlist in 3 weeks of alpha​) and investors poured money into the space. By 2024, it felt like every new week brought a new AI coding product. Companies that initially might have thought “we’ll wait and see” began to fear missing out on a transformative tech shift. Canva launching an AI code generator before design-rival Figma is a telling example (one tech observer quipped they “did not have Canva launching this before Figma on my bingo card”). In brief, no one wants to be last to the AI party – whether it’s a core software company or a software-adjacent platform.

  • Maturation of AI tech: A more technical reason: it became relatively easy to create an AI code tool. Thanks to APIs from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, etc., even a small startup can plug a powerful LLM into their interface and focus on the user experience around it. The heavy lifting (training the AI on billions of lines of code) is done by the AI providers. Bolt.new, for instance, is essentially a friendly UI wrapped around an AI agent and a cloud IDE/runtime. This low barrier to entry led to a Cambrian explosion of experiments – some tools will thrive, many will die, but the collective innovation has accelerated. As one AI dev commented, “it’s amazing what most of them can do – I just created a small synthesizer app with Lovable in minutes”​. That kind of anecdote was unheard of a few years ago. Now, thanks to ubiquitous AI APIs, the difference between a “toy project” and a disruptive product can be just good design and timing.

All these factors have propelled the trend at breakneck speed. By 2025, we will have design software (Canva), cloud platforms (AWS), framework providers (Vercel), indie startups (Cursor, Lovable, etc.), and big tech incumbents (Microsoft, Google) all building similar AI development assistants. It’s reminiscent of the early web rush – everyone knew it would be big, nobody wanted to be left behind. And much like the web, this generative AI coding wave could fundamentally reshape the industry.

From Code to Concepts: The Changing Nature of Software Development

What does this flood of AI coding tools mean for the trajectory of software development itself? In the short term, we’re seeing a shift in how software is created – and who can create it. In the longer term, it could upend the structure of the software business. Let’s break down the implications:

Technological Shift: Developers as “AI Conductors”

The role of software developers is evolving. With AI generating larger portions of code, human programmers are moving towards higher-level orchestration and problem definition. As Microsoft CTO Kevin Scott put it, by 2030 AI could be generating “up to 95% of code,” and engineers will function more like “instruction guides” for the AI​. This doesn’t mean programmers disappear; instead, they delegate routine coding to AI and focus on architecture, integrating components, and ensuring the final product meets requirements. A recent academic paper similarly imagines a 2030 “HyperAssistant” that handles not only coding, but also tasks like debugging, optimization, and even reminding developers to take breaks​​ – essentially an AI project manager paired with an AI coder. In that vision, developers collaborate with AI on all aspects of the development lifecycle.

Even today, we see glimmers of this future. At Google, over 25% of new code is now written by AI systems and then reviewed by human engineers​. That’s a massive chunk of coding already offloaded to machines. GitHub’s CEO noted that Copilot (the AI code assistant) was generating on average 46% of developers’ code as of mid-2023, and that number has likely grown. The consensus in the industry is that AI will handle the bulk of the grunt work, allowing humans to do more creative and complex tasks – or simply to build more with the same effort. One might say we’re moving from “writing code” to “steering code.” The human provides direction, context, and critical oversight; the AI writes the boilerplate, the repetitive logic, and even suggests solutions to harder problems.

Crucially, AI is still a complement rather than a full replacement in development, at least for now. “We emphasize AI as a complementary force, augmenting developers’ capabilities rather than replacing them,” write researchers in a 2024 paper on AI in coding​. This augmentation means a single developer can achieve far more. We’re already hearing stories of tiny teams or solo devs building apps that would have taken a whole squad before. As AI coding becomes standard, the bottleneck shifts from coding effort to imagination and design. In other words, what to build (and why) becomes a more salient question than how to build it, since the “how” is increasingly handled by versatile AI helpers.

Economic Impact: Will Software Companies Dissolve or Evolve?

One of the most provocative questions is whether all these AI tools will eventually erode the need for traditional software companies. If an individual can conjure software on-demand, why purchase off-the-shelf solutions or subscriptions? Why hire a team of developers if one person with AI can do the job of ten? The scenario isn’t far-fetched for certain categories of software. We’re already seeing entrepreneurs use AI builders to replicate basic versions of popular apps. For instance, an AI enthusiast used Bolt.new to clone core features of Spotify and Airbnb as a proof of concept. It’s no wonder some are questioning the future of SaaS (Software-as-a-Service) products. “I’ve been using coding assistant tools like Lovable and am seriously starting to wonder about the viability of legacy SaaS platforms,” one startup founder mused​. The logic: if anyone can spin up a custom CRM, project manager, or website with a few prompts, the moat that many software companies rely on (technical expertise and development time) narrows dramatically.

However, declaring the death of software companies is likely premature. Several counter-forces will preserve the need for professional software products:

  • Quality, polish, and maintenance: While AI can generate an app quickly, making a robust, secure, and user-friendly app is another matter. Companies invest in refining the user experience, fixing edge-case bugs, providing support, and continuously updating features. The average individual may not want to maintain their DIY app long-term (just as many people could technically cook every meal at home but still eat out for convenience and quality). Software companies can offer reliability, accountability, and polish that ad-hoc generated apps might lack, at least until AI can also maintain and perfect apps over time.

  • Complex integrated systems: For simple tools (forms, websites, simple databases), an AI-generated solution might suffice. But for large-scale, complex systems – think enterprise software, high-performance computing, or infrastructure software – you still need coordinated development efforts and expertise. It will be a long time before an AI can single-handedly architect, say, an entire banking system or an operating system via one user’s prompts. Companies building complex software will likely continue, though their internal processes will be augmented by AI. In fact, the companies themselves will use AI to cut costs and improve output, rather than vanish. In economic terms, we might see productivity gains leading to smaller teams, but those teams can then take on more ambitious projects (or simply produce software more cheaply). The net effect could be more software overall, not less business.

  • Innovation and new demands: Paradoxically, making software creation easier can increase demand for professional software. How so? When everyone can have custom apps, expectations rise for what software can do. New ideas flood the market. Professional developers (and companies) will be needed to take the best AI-generated prototypes and turn them into polished products, or to tackle entirely new categories of applications enabled by AI. In other words, AI may automate away routine development, but free humans to focus on cutting-edge innovation, spawning new startups and products we haven’t even imagined. The history of technology shows that automation often shifts jobs rather than obliterating them – old roles disappear, new ones emerge. We may witness a similar transition: fewer coders grinding out CRUD apps, more product designers, AI trainers, data curators, and integrators.

That said, the structure of the software industry could change. We might see fewer large software companies dominating a market when users can easily generate tailored solutions. Instead, there may be marketplaces of AI-generated app templates, or companies might pivot to primarily providing AI platforms and infrastructure rather than finished apps. For example, Canva’s move hints at a possible future where the platform (with AI capabilities) is king, and the specific content (apps/designs) is user-created. Likewise, AWS’s generative App Studio suggests AWS might host tons of custom-generated apps for enterprises, rather than enterprises buying one-size-fits-all software. An analyst speculated that “the line between app creation and app listing might soon blur, ushering in an era where apps are conceived, built, and deployed in one seamless ecosystem — all powered by AI.”​ In such an era, a company’s value might be in running that ecosystem (e.g. AWS, or an app store of AI creations) rather than selling individual app licenses.

From a social perspective, if “everyone can code” (through AI), we could see a burst of grassroots innovation. More small businesses and individuals will create niche tools for their communities or workflows. Empowerment of the individual creator is a likely outcome – similar to how Canva empowered non-designers to create graphics that rival professional work, AI coding tools could enable non-developers to solve local problems with software. This democratization is exciting, but it also requires digital literacy: individuals need to learn how to effectively communicate their needs to AI (prompt engineering) and to evaluate AI outputs. There’s a learning curve and a mindset shift (“Yes, you can build your own tool!”) that may take time to diffuse through society.

In summary, software companies won’t vanish overnight, but they will evolve. Those that embrace AI to deliver more value will thrive; those that rely on old models may struggle as the baseline capability of users rises. We may see more one-person software “companies” powered by AI, offering solutions without a large staff – effectively, the “company” is a person plus their AI agents. We’ll also see established companies doubling down on what AI alone can’t easily provide: vision, brand trust, enterprise support, integration services, and cutting-edge originality.

Expert Insights: The Future of AI-Assisted Software Creation

It’s not just armchair speculation – many experts and industry leaders are actively projecting the future of programming in the age of AI. Here are a few striking insights and projections that shed light on where we’re headed:

  • Kevin Scott (Microsoft CTO) – as mentioned, he envisions that in five years the vast majority of code (up to 95%) could be AI-generated​. He quickly clarified this doesn’t render human developers obsolete; rather, it means developers will supervise AI and focus on the tricky parts. Essentially, coding will shift to “language-in” – humans describe the intent, AI writes the precise syntax. Microsoft is betting big on this vision (see: GitHub Copilot, Azure’s AI services) and sees it as fundamentally altering software development.

  • Google’s experience – Google revealed that more than 1/4 of new code is now written with AI assistance across their company​. This real-world data point confirms that AI coding at scale isn’t just hype; it’s happening inside top tech firms. Google’s developers use internal AI tools (and some public ones) to generate boilerplate and even some complex code, then review and refine it. The fact that a quarter of code can be reliably handled by AI today implies that fraction will grow as models improve. Google’s engineering culture is famously high-standard, so AI passing muster for 25% of code is significant.

  • Academic research – A group of computer science researchers predicted in 2024 that by 2030, advanced AI “HyperAssistants” will cut developers’ effective workload in half​. They outline how AI will move beyond just code generation to integrated support (design suggestions, test generation, coordination tasks)​. This aligns with a broader view that every aspect of software development can be supported by AI: requirement gathering (AI turning natural language from stakeholders into specs), coding (we see that now), testing (AI generating test cases or doing formal verification), deployment (AI optimizing infrastructure), and even team communication. The outcome is not necessarily fewer developers, but developers delivering twice as much value.

  • Bain & Company / McKinsey – Consulting firms have published optimistic outlooks on AI in software. McKinsey projected that future AI pair-programmers could save $100 billion+ in software development costs annually industry-wide, and significantly alleviate the global shortage of developers. Bain’s survey, as cited earlier, showed tangible efficiency boosts already​. These firms advise enterprises to adopt AI dev tools quickly to stay competitive, predicting those who do so will outpace those who don’t.

  • Startup investors – Venture capitalists are extremely bullish. One VC noted that “for the first time in history, we have a technology [genAI] that can make more of itself – software that creates software”. This self-referential power means the growth could be exponential. Investors often reference Andrej Karpathy’s concept of “Software 2.0,” where much code is written by neural networks rather than humans. We’re witnessing Software 2.0’s coming-of-age now, and investors expect entirely new business models to emerge from it.

  • OpenAI’s perspective – OpenAI (creators of GPT-4) have hinted that coding may be one of the first domains where AI achieves superhuman performance. GPT-4 already passed difficult coding interviews and challenges that many human devs struggle with. OpenAI’s newer models (like the hypothetical “GPT-5” or others) might not only write code but also conceptualize program architecture from scratch given high-level goals. Sam Altman (OpenAI CEO) suggested that in the not-too-distant future, developing software might look more like having a conversation with an AI about what the software should do, and iterating until it’s right – effectively eliminating the manual translation from idea to code.

  • Community sentiment – On forums like Reddit, many developers report that 2023–2024 was the tipping point: “I ask all the software companies I meet about [AI-written code]. The number is rarely lower than 40%. For some young programmers it’s 90%,” one industry observer noted​. That is, a lot of new code (especially by junior devs) is being generated by AI and then tweaked. There’s an adjustment period – developers need new skills (prompting AI, validating outputs) – but those who embrace AI are seeing huge productivity leaps. Meanwhile, some skeptics caution about over-reliance: AI can introduce bugs or insecure code if not carefully reviewed. This has led to calls for AI coding best practices and AI literacy as core parts of software engineering education going forward.

If we synthesize these insights, the trajectory becomes clear: we are moving toward a world where creating software is faster, cheaper, and more accessible than ever before. The ratio of human creativity to machine labor in code will skew heavily toward the machine doing the rote work. Developers will still be in the loop, but their jobs will be more about guiding, supervising, and innovating – less about typing out boilerplate logic.

Critically, this transformation in software creation is a microcosm of a larger trend: AI isn’t just taking over manual labor; it’s taking over intellectual labor and amplifying it. Which brings us to a broader and perhaps even more profound question: beyond apps and websites, how might AI’s ability to generate knowledge and guidance affect human development at large? If AI can write code, can it also teach us and make us smarter? Let’s explore the idea of AI not just as a coder, but as a tutor – and how that could elevate human intelligence over generations.

A Future of AI Tutors and a Smarter Human Race?

While AI is rewriting the rules of software engineering, another revolution is quietly brewing in education. AI-powered tutors and assistants have begun to demonstrate dramatic improvements in learning outcomes, suggesting that the same technology making it easier to create apps could also make it easier to create educated humans. This raises a bold possibility: as AI tutors proliferate, humanity’s collective education level could soar. Some even speculate that in a few decades, the average person’s knowledge or cognitive skill might rival that of today’s PhD holders. It sounds fantastical – perhaps even utopian – but let’s examine the trends that underpin such a claim.

Evidence of AI’s impact on learning is already emerging. A study at Harvard in 2024 provided a controlled look at AI tutoring: students in an introductory physics class who had access to an AI tutor learned “more than twice as much in less time” compared to those with only traditional instruction​. That is a staggering result – effectively a >2x learning efficiency gain. The AI tutor (a chatbot assistant) could provide instant, personalized feedback and answer questions 24/7, something even the best human professors cannot do for hundreds of students. Similarly, at MIT and other universities, coding classes that integrated AI assistants saw students completing projects in a fraction of the usual time, without sacrificing understanding.

Sal Khan, the founder of Khan Academy, is so convinced of AI’s potential that he wrote a 2024 book Brave New Words: How AI Will Revolutionize Education. Khan predicts AI will “provide every student with a virtual personalized tutor at an affordable cost,” driving radically improved achievement for all​. Khan Academy has already deployed “Khanmigo,” an AI tutor using GPT-4, to thousands of students with promising results​. Vibe Coding, Part 2: Inside the AI-Powered Coding Revolution

I remember the exact moment vibe coding clicked for me. What Does “Vibe Coding” Mean?

Let’s start with a definition. Vibe coding is a term coined in early 2025 by AI luminary Andrej Karpathy​​. He described it as a new way to write software: you “fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists.” In practice, vibe coding means using conversational natural language prompts to guide large language model (LLM) coding assistants, rather than writing every line yourself.

When Karpathy first talked about it, he painted a vivid picture. He would speak to an AI-powered code editor (using voice commands, no less) and ask for “the dumbest things” like “decrease the padding on the sidebar by half” – tasks he used to do manually but now was too lazy to hunt for in code​. The AI (in his case, an IDE called Cursor paired with a model nicknamed “Sonnet”) would oblige, generating and modifying code on the fly. He’d hit “Accept All” on the changes without even reading the diffs. If a bug popped up or an error message appeared, he’d just paste it back to the AI and say, “Hey, fix this.” More often than not, the AI fixed it or at least tried alternative solutions.

“I’m building a project or web app, but it’s not really coding – I just see stuff, say stuff, run stuff, and copy-paste stuff, and it mostly works,” Karpathy admitted​simonwillison.net.

That, in essence, is vibe coding: treating coding like a conversation. You tell the computer what you want in plain language (the “vibe”), and the AI figures out the how. You don’t sweat the small stuff or obsess over each semicolon. You ride the wave of the AI’s exponential improvements in code generation. It’s coding, Jim, but not as we know it.

Crucially, vibe coding isn’t just using AI to assist coding – it’s a mindset of almost completely trusting the AI to write the code, while you guide and prod it along. In traditional coding, we write and then maybe get AI suggestions; in vibe coding, the AI writes and we suggest. It flips the script.

So, the ecosystem of models is diverse and growing more so.

Vibe-Friendly Development Platforms: Alongside the raw models, we have a crop of specialized tools and environments that make the vibe coding experience smoother. These are worth calling out:

  • Cursor: If vibe coding has an IDE of choice, Cursor might be it. Cursor is an AI-native code editor that gained notoriety after Karpathy praised it. I’ve been using Cursor for some projects – it basically looks like a regular coding environment, but you have an AI chat sidebar deeply integrated with your code. You can highlight a block of code and say, “Hey, what does this do?” or “Refactor this to use hooks instead of classes,” and it’ll do it. More importantly for vibe coding, you can describe high-level changes (like UI tweaks or new features) and Cursor’s AI will attempt to implement them across your codebase. It also has a voice input (through something called “SuperWhisper”), which means you can literally talk to it while your hands stay off the keyboard. That voice interaction is a trip – it feels like pair programming with an invisible partner. Cursor uses whatever model you hook it up to (GPT-4, Claude, etc.), acting as the friendly UI and context manager. Many vibe coders use Cursor to stay “in flow,” because you’re never leaving your editor to ask the AI for something – it’s all in one place, conversational and code-aware.

  • Replit Ghostwriter: Replit is a popular online coding platform, and their Ghostwriter AI is like vibe coding on the web. Replit’s approach is making AI-assisted development accessible through just a browser – no heavy IDE needed. I’ve tried Ghostwriter on small Python and JavaScript projects: you start typing, and it autocompletes whole functions (like GitHub Copilot does), but you can also open a chat with it to ask for bigger tasks. For instance, I typed # TODO: implement user login in code, and then in Ghostwriter’s chat I wrote “Implement the user login function using JWT,” and it generated the function for me, integrated with my codebase. What’s neat is Replit allows real-time collaboration, so theoretically a human and an AI (Ghostwriter) and maybe another human can all be coding simultaneously in the same project. It blurs the lines between human and AI contributions. Replit has a huge community of young coders and learners, so vibe coding techniques are spreading there quickly. I sometimes browse Replit forums and see beginners sharing AI-generated snippets proudly, essentially saying “look what the AI helped me build!”

  • Vercel’s v0: V0.dev (pronounced “Vee-Zero”) is an experimental platform from Vercel that focuses on building web UIs and front-ends through natural language. I gave v0 a whirl and was impressed: you describe a webpage or component, and it produces the corresponding React/Vue code and a live preview. It’s almost like a design tool where the “design input” is your words. For example, I typed: “Create a navbar with a logo on the left and three links on the right (Home, About, Contact). Make the navbar sticky and add a drop shadow.” V0 generated a React component with TailwindCSS that matched the description, and I could see the preview instantly. The cool part was I could then drag and adjust the elements in the preview, and the underlying code would update – a blend of visual and language-based editing. V0’s strength is front-end prototyping; some developers use it to get the UI in place, then switch to another tool or manual coding for the backend. It’s very much aligned with vibe coding’s ethos: fast iteration from idea to interface. I recall reading a guide that recommended “have V0 make the UI layout first, then import into your IDE of choice”​. That’s a workflow I can relate to – let the AI handle the pixel pushing and boilerplate, then you focus on the business logic.

  • Lovable AI: One of the hottest new tools is Lovable (accessible at lovable.dev). The tagline is essentially “build software 20× faster without writing code.” I’ve been playing with Lovable and it’s almost scary how far it goes. It presents a simple prompt box: “What do you want to build?” You then describe your app idea in a paragraph or two – the more detail, the better. I tested it with: “I need a web app for a personal cookbook. Users can add recipes with ingredients and instructions, upload a photo, and search through their recipes by ingredient or title. Include user accounts so my family can login and share recipes.” I hit enter, half-expecting an error or something generic. Instead, Lovable generated a multi-file project: a backend (Node.js) with API endpoints for recipes and user auth, a front-end (React) with pages for recipe listing, viewing, and adding, and even a simple database schema for MongoDB. It packaged this as a downloadable project. I was floored. The code was relatively boilerplate-y (not something a senior engineer would hand-craft), but it was functionally all there. Essentially a complete MVP of my cookbook idea in one go. Lovable is gaining traction especially among non-developers who have ideas but lack coding skills – it tries to make app creation as straightforward as writing a spec. There’s a lively discussion around it: some devs are worried tools like Lovable will “steal our jobs” (its marketing literally brags about doing what teams of developers do), while others see it as just another form of no-code. Either way, it’s an impressive showcase of what an AI can build from just a written concept​.

  • Bolt (by StackBlitz): Bolt.new is another rising star in the vibe coding toolkit, particularly for full-stack web apps. It’s actually built by the team behind StackBlitz (a well-known online IDE), but Bolt is more “agentic” – meaning it doesn’t just provide static suggestions; it actively executes tasks for you. I used Bolt to create a simple event listing web app. The experience was like having an all-in-one development agent: I told Bolt what I wanted, and it generated code for the front-end and back-end, set up a SQLite database, and even spun up a dev server to show me the result. Bolt has a neat trick where it can integrate with external services – for example, I could say, “Also, add Stripe payments for premium events” and since Bolt has some Stripe integration, it scaffolded code to connect to Stripe’s API. It can deploy apps too: after the app was working locally, I clicked “Deploy” and Bolt pushed it to a cloud hosting (with a URL ready to share). This blew my mind: from idea to live app, all within Bolt, using mostly natural language and a few button clicks. It’s the closest I’ve felt to having an “AI engineer” that not only codes but also runs and deploys the project. No wonder Bolt and similar tools have been magnets for VC funding – they’re shaping up to be the next major productivity platforms for developers​.

  • Windsurf (by Codeium): I mentioned Windsurf earlier in the VR example. Windsurf is essentially Codeium’s AI-powered IDE that’s built for vibe coding workflows. What sets it apart is its emphasis on a seamless feedback loop: you can see your app live in a preview pane, interact with it, and then tell the AI to change things you see. For instance, in Windsurf I loaded a web app preview and literally clicked on a button in the preview and said, “Make this button red and increase its padding,” and the AI identified the corresponding code and changed the CSS accordingly. It’s almost uncanny – like the tool reads your mind through your clicks. Windsurf also supports multi-file context, running commands, and deploying straight from the IDE​. Codeium’s aim is clearly to make the entire development cycle AI-assisted: write code, run it, test it, debug it, deploy it, all with an AI copilot deeply integrated. I’ve found Windsurf particularly useful for debugging vibe-coded projects: when something breaks, you can hit a button to “send error to AI” and it analyzes the error log or stack trace and suggests a fix​. It’s like pair programming with a built-in debugger that actually knows your codebase. I suspect we’ll see more of this “see it, shape it, ship it” style in future IDEs – because it lowers the friction between having an idea, testing it live, and getting it out to users.

And beyond these, there are many other tools and plugins: from Visual Studio Code’s Copilot Chat extension to JetBrains AI Assistant, to niche ones like Fig AI for terminal commands. The landscape is rich and evolving. The unifying theme is that AI is being woven into every developer tool, enabling this vibe-led workflow. Where we used to write code, now we increasingly describe and direct.

When AI Joins Your Team: Developers’ Evolving Role

As someone who’s been writing code for over a decade, adopting vibe coding has been both exhilarating and humbling. Exhilarating because I can build faster than ever; humbling because it’s changed the way I think about my own role in the process. Let’s talk about how the role of developers (and creators in general) is evolving in this new paradigm.

From Code Author to Code Curator: Traditionally, a developer’s job is to write code – to be the author crafting each line. In vibe coding, the AI does a lot of that authorship. I’ve felt my own focus shift from writing to guiding and curating. Now, I spend more time deciding what to build and reviewing what the AI wrote, rather than typing it out myself. It’s as if I’ve become an editor and architect, and the AI is the one banging out the first draft. This requires a different mindset: I frame problems for the AI in clear language (prompt engineering is a skill!), and when it responds with code, I inspect that code not line-by-line as I would if I wrote it, but at a higher level. I ask, “Does this do roughly what we need? Are there obvious errors or risky bits?” If it’s mostly right, I accept it and move on. If not, I give feedback to the AI: “This isn’t handling the edge case of X, please fix that,” or sometimes I’ll say “Scrap this approach and try a different technique.” In a way, I’m like a project manager assigning tasks to a very smart but occasionally wayward developer (the AI). I’ve had to let go of the ego of being the one who personally codes every solution. Instead, I take pride in orchestrating a good solution via the AI. It’s a different kind of satisfaction when something works and you know you collaborated with an AI to get there.

The Rise of Prompt Engineering: Writing good prompts has become an art. The difference between a mediocre AI output and an excellent one often comes down to how you ask. I’ve learned to be explicit in my instructions to AI: specify the tech stack I want, give examples of input/output if possible, and even mention constraints (“make sure to handle up to 1000 concurrent users” or “the code should follow our style conventions”). Sometimes I even write prompts that mimic a spec document. For example, instead of “make a chat feature,” I’ll say: “Implement a chat feature with these requirements: 1) Real-time updates using websockets, 2) Store chat history in database, 3) Include timestamps, 4) Provide a ‘typing…’ indicator.” The difference is night and day – the AI’s output for a detailed prompt like that is far closer to what I want, often with minimal corrections needed. It feels very much like writing a thorough ticket for a teammate. We joke that the hottest new programming language is English, and there’s truth to that: clear written communication is becoming a core developer skill. I’ve even seen job postings looking for “AI developer” or “prompt engineer” roles where one key responsibility is crafting prompts and workflows for AI coding systems. It’s a bit surreal, but also logical – in vibe coding, knowing how to talk to the machine is as important as knowing how to talk to other humans in a team.

Quality Assurance and Testing Are Critical: A big chunk of a developer’s evolving role is being the quality filter. If you let the AI code freely without oversight, things can go off the rails. I’ve had AI-written code that looked perfectly confident but contained subtle bugs or security issues. For example, an AI might introduce a SQL query that’s vulnerable to injection if not careful, or it might use an inefficient approach that doesn’t scale. Part of my new job is to review and test the AI’s output thoroughly. I always remind myself (and any juniors experimenting with vibe coding): trust, but verify. These AIs can produce incorrect or nonsensical code with the same smiling confidence as correct code. So you need a human in the loop who understands the problem domain enough to catch mistakes. Personally, I write more unit tests now, and sometimes I even ask the AI to generate those tests (it will happily produce tests for the code it wrote, which is interesting). When an AI-generated function passes a robust test suite I crafted, I start to feel more at ease. In vibe coding teams, I foresee a possible shift where some members focus primarily on writing tests and validation scripts, effectively “guarding” the quality while others focus on prompting the AI to add features. The dynamic is like one person rapidly building, another person rigorously testing – except the builder is half-automated.

Debugging by Conversation: Debugging has changed too. In the past, I’d stare at a stack trace, dig through code, maybe add some print statements. Now, when something breaks, my instinct is: ask the AI. I copy the error message and say, “The app crashed with this error. What might be the cause?” Nine times out of ten, the AI points me in the right direction or fixes it outright. It’s like having a debugger that can analyze the problem with you. However, if the AI itself caused the bug by writing flawed code, it sometimes struggles to fix what it doesn’t fully understand. In those cases, I have to step in with my own knowledge. One weird thing I’ve noticed: vibe coding forces you to maintain some level of understanding of the codebase, even if you didn’t write it all. Because if something’s really off, you’re the final authority to diagnose it. I had a bug the AI couldn’t solve – it kept applying band aid fixes that didn’t address the root cause. I ended up reading through the code (a chunk of AI-generated logic for caching results) and discovered a fundamental flaw. I then explained to the AI what the flaw was, and only then could it properly fix it. This taught me that while vibe coding can spare you from writing a lot of code, it doesn’t spare you from thinking deeply about that code’s behavior. In fact, you may need to think more in order to guide an AI which doesn’t truly understand the problem the way you do. So yes, I debug differently now – often via dialogue – but I also keep my classical debugging skills sharp for those times when I need to outthink the machine.

Architect, Designer, Visionary: Perhaps the most gratifying change is that I get to focus more on the creative and high-level aspects of software. Since I spend less energy on rote coding, I can allocate more brainpower to the app’s design, the user experience, and new ideas. It reminds me of moving from being an individual contributor to a tech lead – you zoom out from the nitty-gritty and look at the whole system. Vibe coding allows even a solo dev to take that higher-level view. For instance, while AI churns out yet another CRUD form, I might sketch how the overall user flow should work, or consider the data model implications of a new feature. I think about edge cases and how to prompt the AI to handle them. In a sense, developers are becoming more like product designers and problem solvers, and the AI is the one translating those solutions into code. I find that enjoyable. It’s the fun part of software development – solving puzzles and imagining features – without as much of the slog of implementation. Of course, many developers (myself included) enjoy programming for its own sake, and vibe coding can feel like it’s stealing the joy of coding. I admit I sometimes miss writing a beautifully optimized algorithm by hand. So I’ll do it manually to scratch that itch. But for a lot of everyday programming tasks, I don’t mind offloading them to AI. It frees up time to be creative and pursue more projects than I could before. Some of my peers have started calling themselves “software directors” – like a film director guiding a crew, we guide the AI crew to produce the software vision we have. It’s a cheesy analogy, but it does feel that way at times.

Collaboration and Communication: The human role also shifts in collaborative settings. If two or more developers are working with AI assistants on a project, communication is key. We have to sync not just with each other, but also ensure we’re instructing our respective AIs in a compatible way. I encountered a scenario where a teammate and I were both vibe coding different modules of an app. My AI was producing code with a certain style and assumptions, and his AI had a slightly different approach. When we merged, things didn’t fit well initially (different database schemas, etc.). We realized we needed to agree on some design choices upfront and then feed those agreements into our AI prompts so that both AIs work toward the same vision. This is new – essentially, we had to do a bit of “prompt documentation” for consistency. Additionally, when he had the AI write a chunk of code, I would review it, and vice versa, much like code review but with the awareness that an AI wrote it. Sometimes I’d ask, “Did you prompt it to consider security for this part?” and if not, we’d re-prompt to harden the code. So human-to-human collaboration now includes discussing how we use the AI, not just the end code. It adds an interesting layer to teamwork, and I think as teams formalize this, we’ll see methodologies or best practices emerge (maybe an agile process where some tickets are explicitly marked for AI execution, etc.).

At the end of the day, I feel developers aren’t so much being replaced as they are evolving. The role is shifting from craftsman to conductor. You still need to deeply understand software engineering principles – arguably even more so, to catch AI’s mistakes – but you apply that knowledge differently. It’s less about hand-coding every line, more about making high-level decisions, coordinating AI efforts, and ensuring the final product meets requirements. For me, it’s been a journey of learning to let go when appropriate and step in when necessary, and always keep learning (because these tools change fast!).

The Buzz in Tech Circles: Hype, Skepticism, and the Future of Coding

No conversation about vibe coding is complete without addressing what everyone in the tech world seems to be asking: Is this the real deal, or just another buzzword? Depending on who you talk to, vibe coding is either the dawn of a new era or just a fancy term for AI-assisted programming with a marketing spin. As someone immersed in this space, I’ve heard it all.

The Hype – “This Changes Everything”: There’s a palpable excitement among many of us that vibe coding hints at the future of how software will be built. Tech influencers on Twitter are calling vibe coding “the next evolution of coding”. Some venture capitalists are even saying things like “If you’re not vibe coding, you’ll be left behind,” echoing what Y Combinator’s CEO Garry Tan said in that discussion​. The optimistic viewpoint is that AI will democratize coding, finally making the longstanding dream of “everyone can code” a reality – except without everyone actually needing to learn code. Just as the move from assembly to high-level languages made programming more accessible, the move from traditional coding to natural language “vibing” could open the floodgates for millions more creators. I’ve had non-engineer colleagues build small tools for themselves using vibe coding, and they were giddy with the empowerment it gave them. That’s a big deal: people solving their own problems with software, without a developer as the middleman. In tech meetups, I often hear references to earlier shifts like the introduction of visual programming or no-code tools, and how this goes beyond, because the AI can handle complexity not possible in simple no-code platforms. True believers see vibe coding as not just a productivity boost for coders, but as a creative renaissance – where the limiting factor becomes one’s imagination, not technical chops. There’s also excitement that this could lead to a boom in software innovation: if prototyping an app or feature is now 10× faster, we can try ten times more ideas, leading to more breakthroughs and niche solutions. The vibe coding community online frequently shares success stories – “I built X in a day, which would have taken me weeks before!” – and it fuels this sense that we’re on the cusp of something revolutionary.

The Skepticism – “Hold On, It’s Not Magic”: On the flip side, many veteran developers and industry observers urge caution. I was chatting with an experienced engineer friend who quipped, “Vibe coding? More like vibe hacking. It’s fine for toy projects, but would you trust it for a pacemaker’s software or a banking system? No way.” This sentiment is echoed by experts like Gary Marcus (a prominent AI critic) who called out that some vibe-coded apps are basically just regurgitating existing code and ideas, not truly innovating​. The concerns break down into a few key points:

First, code quality and maintainability. AI-generated code can be messy under the hood. It might work initially but be a nightmare to maintain. Imagine inheriting a large codebase that was vibe-coded – with the original “developer” not really knowing why the AI wrote things a certain way. Professional devs worry about technical debt: are we trading short-term speed for long-term pain? A common refrain: If you didn’t write it, you may struggle to debug or extend it. I’ve felt this myself when revisiting an AI-written module months later; I had to re-learn what it was doing, almost as if I was reading someone else’s code (because I was – the “someone else” just happened to be an AI).

Second, reliability and correctness. We know AI can make mistakes, sometimes dumb ones, sometimes subtle ones. In a production system, those mistakes could be costly. A security bug introduced by an AI could open a hole for hackers. As one developer advocate put it, “You can’t just let the robots run wild without checking their work, or you’ll get yourself into trouble”​. Many in the field stress that vibe coding needs a solid safety net: code reviews, tests, oversight by someone with technical expertise. The Guardian wrote an article basically saying “you don’t need code to be a programmer now, but you still need expertise”​​ – that captures it well.

Third, the knowledge gap for newbies. While vibe coding lowers the entry barrier, some argue it might give a false sense of competence. A beginner might string together an app via AI, but if they never learn why the code works, they could be in trouble when things go off-script. I’ve seen this firsthand: a friend with minimal coding background built a working web app with AI, but when it started crashing due to a subtle bug, he was totally lost. If the AI couldn’t fix it, he had no idea what to do. It’s like someone who learned to drive with an AI co-pilot always holding the wheel – when the co-pilot steps away, can they handle the road alone? Some experienced devs see this as dangerous: junior engineers might skip learning fundamentals, which are still needed to truly ensure software is robust. An AI advocate might respond that maybe the AI co-pilot will never step away. But in any non-trivial project, it eventually will hit a limit or error where human insight is needed. As AI researcher Simon Willison nicely put it: if you carefully review and understand all the AI’s code, then you’re just using AI as an assistant, not vibe coding per say – implying that true vibe coding (where you don’t understand all the code) is something to be very wary of, especially for production systems.

Fourth, ethics and originality. There’s a debate about whether AI-generated code is truly “new” or just remixing training data. Karpathy and others have noted that as long as it works, maybe it doesn’t matter how it was made​. But some critics like Gary Marcus (again) point out that being impressed by an AI spitting out a known solution isn’t the same as human creativity or understanding​. There’s also the matter of attribution – if an AI regurgitated code that was in its training set (say, a well-known open-source library function), are vibe coders unknowingly plagiarizing? The legal and licensing questions around AI-generated code are still unresolved. Companies are treading carefully; I know of at least one big tech firm that restricts using AI for code unless the model is known to be trained on proper licensed data, because they fear a lawsuit down the line claiming IP infringement. So while vibe coding is fun and productive, the professionalization of it will require navigating these issues.

In tech circles, this mix of hype and skepticism often leads to lively (sometimes heated) discussions. I’ve sat in on engineering meetings where half the team was excited to incorporate more AI, and the other half was pushing back hard, raising all the aforementioned concerns. One senior architect said something like, “Just because Karpathy can vibe out a weekend project doesn’t mean our team can ship mission-critical code that way.” That’s a fair point. The consensus that seems to be emerging is that vibe coding is excellent for prototyping, exploration, and personal projects, and it’s gradually finding its place in production workflows with proper checks. But full-on vibe coding (as originally defined, where you don’t even look at the code) is not widely adopted for serious, large-scale software yet – nor should it be, until the tools and practices mature.

Signals for the Future: Where is this all heading? I often ask myself that as I alternate between marveling at what I can do now and cringing at the rough edges that still exist.

In a way, vibe coding feels like a glimpse of what coding might look like in 5 or 10 years. Today, it’s at the cutting edge – early adopters and enthusiasts are showing it off, some companies are dipping their toes. If the trend continues, tomorrow it might become mainstream. I envision a future IDE (maybe a successor to VS Code or something entirely new) where the default mode is conversational. Instead of opening a new file and typing, you’d create a new module by saying, “Hey AI, I need a module for managing user profiles with these fields… go ahead and set that up.” The AI does the boilerplate, then you refine. Writing code by hand might become a niche activity, reserved for special low-level optimizations or pleasure, much like writing assembly is today.

It also signals that the abstraction level of programming is rising. We went from machine code to assembly to high-level languages, and now to natural language. Each jump made programming more accessible and powerful, though it also abstracted away some control. Vibe coding is the next jump – code at the level of ideas and intent. This could unlock programming to millions who were shut out before. I find that inspiring: imagine artists, scientists, doctors, anyone with domain knowledge but not coding skills, being able to create software to solve their problems by just explaining what they need. We see hints of this already – an ecologist friend of mine described an analysis tool they wanted and basically vibe-coded a rough version with GPT, something they’d been asking their IT department to help with for ages.

Another future angle is the convergence of AI agents. Right now, vibe coding typically involves one AI assisting one developer. But there are projects experimenting with multiple AI “agents” that can coordinate – one might generate code, another might generate tests for that code, another might check for security issues, all orchestrated by a master agent. This multi-agent approach could result in a sort of autonomous coding assembly line (there was buzz about systems like “AutoGPT” that attempt autonomy). It’s not mature yet – often these agents get confused or stuck in loops – but it hints at future tools where you spin up an entire virtual software team: “Architect AI, give me a design; Coder AI, implement it; Tester AI, verify it; DevOps AI, deploy it.” As a human, you’d oversee the collaboration and step in when needed. It sounds wild, but not unimaginable given current trajectories.

Crucially, I think the human role will remain vital. The nature of it will change, as we discussed. If vibe coding truly becomes dominant, being a great software creator might be less about knowing every syntax quirk and more about having strong problem-solving skills, domain understanding, and an intuition for guiding AI effectively. It might also require more soft skills – communication, coordinating between stakeholders and AI, etc. Perhaps coding will be seen less as an engineering discipline and more as a design/communication discipline. That’s a big shift.

In tech circles, there’s also talk about how this affects jobs and the industry. Will we need fewer programmers? Or will we simply tackle bigger challenges with the same number of people? Historically, when tools get better, ambition grows. We don’t see companies laying off all frontend devs because we got React or laying off all ops engineers because we got cloud automation – instead, we build more complex systems. Similarly, vibe coding might not shrink the software workforce; it could just mean we aim higher, build software we couldn’t before, or address bespoke needs that weren’t worth the cost previously. That said, the skill profile may change. Junior devs might need to adapt quickly – the duties of an “entry-level coder” in 2030 could be very different from today. Universities and bootcamps are already scrambling to include AI coding in curricula, which is telling.

Finally, vibe coding’s rise is stirring philosophical questions: If the act of coding becomes as simple as telling a story, does the meaning of “being a programmer” change? Many programmers (myself included) identify with the craft of coding. It’s creative and logical and has a certain flow state. If that grunt work diminishes, some worry the job might become less satisfying – “all the fun parts will be done by AI, and I’m just left babysitting it.” I empathize with this sentiment, but I also find that new joys emerge. There’s a certain magic in collaborating with an AI – when it surprises you with a clever solution or saves you hours, it’s delightful. And you can always dip back into hands-on coding when you want; vibe coding doesn’t forbid that. In the end, I suspect coding will bifurcate: routine programming might largely be automated, while high-stakes or highly creative programming will remain a human art (with AI as a tool). This could elevate the overall level of innovation, as developers channel their energy into the really hard or novel problems.

As I wrap up this deep dive, I’ll share a personal reflection: Vibe coding has rekindled some childlike wonder in me. I feel like I’m in a sci-fi novel, commanding a computer with my voice and it just does things. It reminds me of when I first learned to code – that feeling of power and possibility, making the computer do whatever I could imagine (within BASIC’s limits, back then!). Now the limits are pushing out further than ever. We’re not quite at “computer, build me an MMO game” and boom it’s done – but we’re inching in that direction.

Will future generations even distinguish between “coding” and just “telling the computer what you need”? Possibly not. As one optimistic colleague declared, “The best programmer 10 years from now may well be someone who never writes a single line of code themselves.” It sounds paradoxical, but it could be true.

For now, vibe coding is our fascinating present and likely our future. It’s altering the fabric of software development in real time. And I, for one, am enjoying the ride – with one hand on the keyboard and the other firmly on the vibe.
