The AI Money-Go-Round: Real Companies, Circular Financing, and the “Hybrid Scam” Question

Nvidia, OpenAI, Anthropic and their cloud partners are building real businesses. Their interlocking investments and infrastructure commitments raise a different question: how much of the apparent demand can sustain itself without another round of financing?

By Micah Blumberg

The defense of the AI investment boom often begins with something undeniable: the chips exist, the data centers are being built, and customers use the software. But that defense leaves a financial question unanswered. What happens when companies selling the infrastructure also invest in its buyers, guarantee their obligations, or promise to purchase capacity that those buyers cannot sell elsewhere?

That is the concern behind what I call a possible “hybrid scam”: a real operating business whose legitimate products and sales provide cover for a misleading investment story. The term is an argument to investigate, not a finding that OpenAI, Anthropic or Nvidia has committed fraud. The evidence establishes interlocking financing and substantial risk. It does not establish that every financed sale is fictitious, or that these companies are pyramid schemes.

The strongest criticism does not require pretending the hardware is imaginary. Real revenue can coexist with an exaggerated impression of independent, self-sustaining demand.

Follow the financing, not just the shipment

Consider a hypothetical supplier that invests $10 billion in a customer. The customer then spends $10 billion buying that supplier’s equipment. The equipment can be real, the investment can acquire a real ownership interest, and the sale can have economic substance. Nevertheless, the purchase does not demonstrate that outside customers have generated $10 billion of demand for the services the equipment will produce.

The supplier has exchanged cash for an investment asset and subsequently received cash from selling equipment. It has not magically earned back its investment risk: it still holds the customer’s equity, which can lose value, while the customer must make the equipment productive. Gross revenue also says nothing by itself about the supplier’s profit after production costs.

If the customer raises money at a higher valuation, its investors may also record an increase in investment value under the applicable accounting treatment. That gain and the equipment sale are different entries. Counting both as evidence of independently proven customer demand would be analytically misleading even when both entries are properly reported.

Nvidia is helping underwrite the market it supplies

Nvidia’s fiscal 2027 second-quarter filing disclosed guarantees capped at $105 billion supporting land, power and shell construction at SB Energy’s Ohio campus for OpenAI-linked leases. The project covers roughly 4.25 gigawatts. The guarantees become effective in phases, with the first expected in fiscal 2029; the site is to use Nvidia infrastructure, subject to limited exceptions. This is contingent exposure, not $105 billion already paid, and its conditions and termination provisions matter. Nvidia quarterly filing

Nvidia’s subsequent fiscal third-quarter CFO commentary identifies $32 billion in conditional neocloud commitments and $118 billion in maximum gross guarantees. It also reports $81 billion in receivables and 64 days sales outstanding, including the effect of extended payment terms for certain investment-grade customers. These disclosures complicate any blanket claim that all reported sales have already been collected in cash. Its $60.1 billion hyperscale and $47.5 billion AI-cloud-and-enterprise revenue split classifies customers, not their ultimate sources of funding. Nvidia CFO commentary

It would therefore be wrong to declare the entire $47.5 billion category circular. It would be equally wrong to treat the hyperscaler category as proof that every purchase was financed by profitable AI end-user demand. Those conclusions require information the category labels do not provide.

The $105 billion project guarantee must not be added to an aggregate guarantee figure as though it were separate spending. Nor should an announced investment, an investment’s current valuation, a purchase commitment and a contingent guarantee be combined into one supposedly equivalent pile of cash.

Amazon shows the investment-and-revenue connection

Amazon’s September 2026 filing reports $50 billion invested in OpenAI and cumulative Anthropic investments of $18 billion. Its commercial relationships include major AWS purchase commitments. A remaining $15 billion Anthropic financing facility is tied to compute-delivery milestones. Amazon also recorded $62.8 billion of upward adjustments to Anthropic preferred stock during the first nine months of 2026, in other income rather than revenue. Amazon quarterly filing

This is the documented version of the concern: one company can be investor, supplier and beneficiary of valuation increases within the same relationship. The filing does not trace each dollar of cloud revenue to a particular invested dollar. It also publicly discloses the ties, which matters when evaluating an allegation that the relationships themselves are concealed.

CoreWeave makes the funding gap visible

CoreWeave reported $5.638 billion of operating cash inflow in the first nine months of 2026, against $23.558 billion in cash purchases of property and equipment. Subtracting the latter from the former leaves a $17.920 billion gap. Financing cash inflow was $21.346 billion. Its filing also records a $2 billion Nvidia equity investment in January 2026. Operating cash generation is real, but it did not fund the infrastructure expansion; customer prepayments also affect operating cash flow. CoreWeave quarterly filing

There is a direct contractual link as well. A September 2025 agreement initially valued at $6.3 billion obligates Nvidia, subject to its terms, to purchase residual unsold CoreWeave capacity through April 2032. An agreement’s headline value is not revenue already earned. But the arrangement demonstrates that a chip supplier can also absorb some of the downstream capacity risk. CoreWeave agreement disclosure

Anthropic’s commitments need their liquidity counterweight

Anthropic’s November 10 amended registration statement reports $900.9 billion in aggregate infrastructure commitments. Approximately $451.6 billion is expected before the end of 2030, with much of the remainder extending through 2045; about $70.7 billion falls due in the twelve months after September 30, 2026. Those amounts are not all debt due in 2028.

The same filing reports $33.2 billion in revenue, a $1.3 billion operating loss and $6.6 billion of positive operating cash flow for the first nine months of 2026. It also reports $131.9 billion in cash and short-term investments and $14.8 billion in available undrawn credit at September 30. Cash and available credit are distinct resources. These disclosures argue against inferring imminent insolvency merely from the next year’s infrastructure payments, while leaving the longer-term economics unresolved. Anthropic amended filing

A serious analysis must include the funding cushion alongside the obligations. It must also avoid counting the same leases and compute arrangements twice when moving between tables, subsequent-event disclosures and headline commitment totals.

The $500 billion-by-2028 claim is not a verified threshold

There is no established requirement in the material reviewed here that each leading lab must earn $500 billion annually by 2028. Revenue needed to support a valuation is a modeling judgment; cash needed to meet a contractual payment is a dated obligation. They cannot be substituted for each other.

Reuters reported in September, citing a company presentation reviewed by the Financial Times, that OpenAI forecast $278 billion of cumulative cash burn across 2026–2030, with revenue projected to rise from $36 billion in 2026 to $350 billion in 2030. Those are attributed projections, not completed results, and they do not establish a $500 billion 2028 revenue hurdle. Reuters report

There are, however, exceptionally ambitious forecasts worth scrutinizing. ARK’s November 2 analysis models combined OpenAI and Anthropic year-end revenue run-rate above $2 trillion in 2028. Its notes explicitly identify assumed demand, utilization and monetization. Its broader opportunity model assumes tenfold knowledge-worker productivity and a software-vendor share of the resulting value. An annualized run-rate is not a full year of recognized revenue. ARK’s model and assumptions

The critical question is whether the projected paying market materializes, at sufficient margins and soon enough. A mathematical projection can be internally consistent while its commercial assumptions fail.

The social-media discussion that prompted this investigation also circulated large reductions attributed to a November 13 SemiAnalysis institutional model update. The underlying update was not independently verified for this article. Its quoted accelerator-capex estimates should not be recast as AI-lab revenue, and the circulating figures are not treated here as established facts.

The strongest counterargument deserves space

Large cloud companies have substantial existing businesses. Microsoft reported $58 billion in operating cash flow and $18.6 billion in free cash flow for its September 2026 quarter. That is important counterevidence to a story in which the entire AI ecosystem exists only by recycling fresh investor money. It does not, however, isolate the return on incremental AI infrastructure. Microsoft earnings discussion

Infrastructure often requires financing before it earns its eventual return. Supplier support can accelerate a useful market. The existence of financing is not the incriminating fact. The danger arises if its role is obscured, its risks are understated, or intermediate purchases are sold to investors as proof of durable final demand.

What would distinguish a boom from a deceptive investment story?

The missing analysis is a reconciliation: who supplied the financing, who received it, what was purchased, what has actually been paid, and how much cash ultimately came from customers outside the financing relationships. Even an unrelated customer may be venture-funded, so independence and sustainability are separate tests.

Investors need clearer disclosure of supplier-funded purchases, guarantees, unused capacity obligations, cloud credits, collection terms, utilization, realized pricing and customer retention. They also need to distinguish revenue from free cash flow, and expansion spending from the replacement spending required as equipment ages or becomes obsolete.

Adding Nvidia’s equipment sales, a cloud provider’s revenue and an AI lab’s revenue does not produce three independent pools of end-user spending. It counts different stages of the same supply chain. Likewise, a high valuation does not provide cash until financing actually closes, and a rising paper investment value cannot pay an electricity bill without being monetized or borrowed against.

Calling the situation a “hybrid scam” expresses a concern that genuine businesses may lend credibility to an investment narrative that outruns economic reality. Establishing an actual scam would require evidence of deception, manipulation or other misconduct beyond expensive infrastructure and optimistic forecasts. An eventual collapse would not, by itself, supply that missing proof.

The burden should not fall only on skeptics to prove the products are fake. They are not. The industry’s burden is to show that enough independent customers will pay enough money to justify the infrastructure being built, without indefinitely relying on the sellers to finance the buyers. Until that is demonstrated, real sales remain evidence of activity, not conclusive evidence of a self-sustaining AI economy.
